# The finance behind a daily price

> Research notes gathered on 2026-10-30 for issue #46 (1.16, daily prices from Factor returns and Treasury yields), with what #47 (dividends and the Sweep rate) must share with it. They inform the decisions but don't make them. Every claim below was read in the cited document, or in data downloaded from its publisher, unless the text says otherwise. A number marked **computed here** was worked out from such data by the method shown, not copied from a publication. The scripts that made them were written in the worktree's `.dev/scratch/` (gitignored, so not committed); each method is described where its number appears.

## Read this first

**What was asked.** Six open questions: how many decimal places a price keeps, how a bond fund earns its daily income, how a Security's own surprise is drawn, how long the resampled blocks of days are, how to correct what the 2020 to 2026 pool does to the long run, and what a Business day is. Each section ends in a **Recommendation**.

**Decided already, and kept.** The pool stays 2020 to 2026. Factors and yield changes are drawn together from the same days. Resampling uses blocks of days with no state, so the same date always gives the same numbers. The long-term fund keeps its duration of 13.57 and its own volatility of 0.025. Draws come from the custodian's `sampling.py` (`stream(label)`, whose `random()` is the one method Python guarantees across versions), with a label of seed, date and identifier, and are calculated in `Decimal` (ADR 0013).

**Terms, once.** Words with a capital letter are in [GLOSSARY.md](../../GLOSSARY.md) (Business day, Return, Total return, Own volatility, Factor return, Sweep rate, Ex-dividend date, NAV, Duration).

- **RF, Mkt-RF, SMB and HML** are the Factor file's four columns: what a one-month loan to the US government earned, how much the market beat that, how much small companies beat big ones, and how much cheap-looking companies beat expensive-looking ones (the primer, Part 2).
- **The pool** is the set of real days that days after the Factor file's end are copied from, in blocks.
- A **par yield** is the yield on the Treasury's published curve: what a Treasury security of that tenor would pay if it sold at exactly its face value. Yields are published in percent, and a yield change in percentage points, but the formulas here take fractions: a change of 0.02 points is 0.0002 in a formula, so with a duration of 5 it moves the price by 0.10%. A yield is not a Return: it's a yearly rate, while a Return here is one day's gain or loss.
- **Carry** is the interest a bond fund earns each day just because time passes, before any change in yields. A fund with a 4% yield earns about 4% / 365 = 0.011% a day. A Return of −Duration × the yield change leaves it out.
- **Accrual** is interest counted as earned day by day, before it is paid. A **day count** is the rule for how many days a period has: _actual/365_ counts the real calendar days and divides by 365, so a Monday after a weekend counts 3 days.
- **Tick** is the smallest step a quoted price may move in (a cent, for most stocks).
- **Autocorrelation** says how much today's value tells you about tomorrow's. 0 means nothing, 1 means everything. Daily stock Returns have almost none. Their sizes do: a big move tends to be followed by another big move (**volatility clustering**).
- **Excess kurtosis** says how heavy the tails of a distribution are: how often a day is far from the average. A normal distribution has 0. A **Student-t** distribution with ν (nu, "degrees of freedom") has more: its excess kurtosis is 6 / (ν − 4) for ν above 4, so ν = 5 gives 6, and fewer degrees of freedom means heavier tails. The mapping ν = 4 + 6 / k turns a measured excess kurtosis k into the ν that has the same number.
- A **block bootstrap** makes a long series by gluing together stretches ("blocks") of days copied from history. Each stretch keeps the real order of days inside it, so patterns that last a few days survive. **L** is the block length in Business days.
- **Demeaning** a series subtracts its average from every value, so it has an average of 0.
- A **percentile band** (p5 to p95) says that 90 of 100 simulated paths ended between the two numbers.
- **Unit variance** means a random draw is scaled so its spread is 1, so multiplying it by a number gives a draw with exactly that spread.
- **÷ √252** turns a yearly volatility into a daily one. If days are independent, their variances add up, so a year's spread is a day's × √252 (about 252 Business days a year), which is 15.9. A yearly own volatility of 0.25 is 0.25 ÷ 15.9 = 1.6% a day.

## Question 1: How many decimal places does a price keep, and how is it rounded?

**What the rules say.** Nothing in the rules that govern an ordinary fund's price names a number of decimal places.

- Rule 2a-4(a) defines a fund's "current net asset value" as an amount that "reflects calculations … made substantially in accordance with" six items, among them "(6) Interest income and other income shall be included to date of calculation." Paragraph (b) is the only mention of a cent: items (a)(4) and (a)(6) "need not be so reflected if cumulatively, when netted, they do not amount to as much as one cent per outstanding share". It sets a threshold, not a number of places. Searching the text of Rule 22c-1 for "round" and "cent" finds nothing about decimals (computed here, a text search of the copy read).
- Rule 2a-7 does name places, but only for money market funds. A money market fund that floats its price "must compute its price per share … by rounding the fund's current net asset value per share to a minimum of the fourth decimal place in the case of a fund with a $1.0000 share price or an equivalent or more precise level of accuracy for money market funds with a different share price (e.g. $10.000 per share, or $100.00 per share)". Its definition of the stable-price alternative says the NAV "is rounded to the nearest one percent" (in a $1 fund, one percent is one cent).
- Rule 612 sets the tick for quotes in stocks and exchange-traded funds, not for a fund's NAV. As in force on 2024-01-01: no bid, offer or order may be priced "in an increment smaller than $0.01 if that bid or offer, order, or indication of interest is priced equal to or greater than $1.00 per share", and "$0.0001 if … priced less than $1.00". The 2024 amendment (89 FR 81774), which is what eCFR shows today, adds a half-cent tier ("$0.005, if the Time Weighted Average Quoted Spread … was equal to or less than $0.015"). Search results say its compliance date was pushed first to 2 November 2026 and then to November 2027; no SEC order was read (see "Still open"). By its text Rule 612 limits quotes, orders and indications of interest, not executions, and a closing price is an execution price (my reading; the SEC's FAQ on this wasn't readable).
- **ADR 0013's "fund prices often carry four"** is supported by exactly one rule, 2a-7, for floating-price money market funds. It isn't a rule for stock, bond or balanced funds.

**What real publishers do (computed here).** State Street's published NAV history for all 12 funds carries 6 decimals on every row (for example SPTL on 2026-10-29: 24.866589, SPY 769.506335). The funds' market prices trade in cents. The SPDR prospectus read says only that distributions of net investment income "are generally declared and paid monthly" and "Expenses and fees, including the management fees, are accrued daily"; the part of the NAV section that was read has no rounding sentence. One search summary claimed an older SPDR prospectus says NAV is "rounded to the nearest cent"; the page text read doesn't contain it, so that isn't relied on. I found no primary source for the ordinary mutual-fund NAVs that Vanguard or Fidelity print (often two decimals); that is unverified.

**Which rounding direction does any source state?** "To the nearest" (Rule 2a-7's two definitions). None states how a tie breaks. ADR 0013 already names half-to-even for aggregates, and its down-rounding is for shares, where "recorded shares never cost more than the cash".

**What rounding does to a price that is built from the day before (computed here).**

The primer's Part 3 stock table, 153.40 on Friday 6 March, with its own Returns (+1.00%, −1.93%, +0.51%, +0.82%, +0.63%), each day's price taken from the day before's rounded one:

| Rounding                     | Mon      | Tue      | Wed      | Thu      | Fri      |
| ---------------------------- | -------- | -------- | -------- | -------- | -------- |
| none                         | 154.9340 | 151.9438 | 152.7187 | 153.9710 | 154.9410 |
| 4 places, half-even          | 154.9340 | 151.9438 | 152.7187 | 153.9710 | 154.9410 |
| 2 places, half-even (primer) | 154.93   | 151.94   | 152.71   | 153.96   | 154.93   |
| 2 places, always down        | 154.93   | 151.93   | 152.70   | 153.95   | 154.91   |

Cents agree with 4 places on the first day and are a cent lower by Friday, as the primer says. Always rounding down, which is the direction ADR 0013 uses for shares, is 0.02% low by Friday.

Then a long path. A bond fund following the 5-year yield (Return = −4.83 × the yield change + carry, by the recipe of Question 2, no dividends, no own surprise) over the 1,673 days of the 2020 to 2026 pool, starting at 10.02, 25.00 and 50.00; the table shows the end price's difference from the unrounded path:

| Rounding            | Start 10.02 | Start 25.00 | Start 50.00 |
| ------------------- | ----------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| 2 places, half-even | +4.8%       | +1.4%       | +0.33%      |
| 2 places, down      | −78.4%      | −31.4%      | −16.3%      |
| 3 places, half-even | −0.55%      | −0.04%      | −0.03%      |
| 4 places, half-even | −0.01%      | −0.002%     | +0.002%     |
| 6 places, half-even | 0.0%        | 0.0%        | 0.0%        |

The half-even figures depend on the exact series of Returns, so read them as sizes: cents cost a $10 fund a few percent, 4 places a hundredth of a percent. For a stock fund (153.40 start, Return = RF + Mkt-RF, no own surprise) cents give −0.10% and "down" gives −3.8%. Why: on a $10.02 fund, half a cent is 0.05% of the price, and 19% of the pool's days have a bond-fund Return smaller than that (9% at $25), so cents round the move away. Each rounding is a random error of up to half the last place, added to every later price (a standard deviation of 0.00003 per day at 4 places, 0.0014 after 2,520 days, 0.014% of a $10 price).

**Recommendation for #46.** Keep every price to **4 decimal places**, rounded to the nearest, **ties to even**, for every Security, and compute each day's price at full `Decimal` precision from the previous stored price.

- Four places is where the cents' damage to a $10 bond fund disappears (−0.01% over 1,673 days; cents gave +4.8%) and where the arithmetic matches ADR 0013's own wording. No rule names the number of places for an ordinary fund, so 4 is a labelled choice. It is also no more than real files carry (6).
- "Nearest, half to even" is the only direction any source states; never round down a price, which drifts it by half a unit a day.
- Evidence against: real stock closing prices are quoted in cents, and cents cost a $153 stock only about 0.1% over the same days; an option is to store stocks at 2 places and funds at 4, which needs a field in the catalog. This note doesn't recommend it, because one rule is simpler and 4 places never hurts.
- Consequences: the primer's Part 3 tables round to cents, so their prices (and the ready-made tests they offer) must be recomputed at 4 places; the stock table above shows the new column. The next day's price is built from the stored, rounded one, so the stored price is what a test must carry forward.

## Question 2: How does a bond fund earn its daily income?

**What real sources say.**

- _A fund's NAV includes accrued income every day._ Rule 2a-4(a)(6): "Interest income and other income shall be included to date of calculation." So a bond fund's price rises a little each day for interest earned and falls by the whole dividend on its Ex-dividend date. SPDR's prospectus: dividends "are generally declared and paid monthly by the Fund" (2011 filing, read as above).
- _A bond index counts income by accrual on calendar days._ Bloomberg's methodology: "Calculating these returns requires daily bond prices, accrued interest calculations, and a record of the timing and amount of coupon and principal payments." Its coupon return "measures the interest income earned by a security, reflecting changes in accrued interest plus any interest paid during that period, divided by the dirty price of the security at the beginning of the period". Its footnote says accrued interest "is calculated using a T+1 calendar day settlement assumption" and that "Using a T+1 calendar day settlement assumption intra-month means that accrued interest as of a Friday business day will not include accrued interest for the weekend. Accrual for the weekend will be reflected on the first business day after the weekend." So interest counts calendar days, and Monday gets three days.
- _Treasury accrues on the actual calendar days._ 31 CFR 356, Appendix B: "We base the daily interest decimal on the actual number of calendar days in the half-year or half-years involved." That is actual/actual, with the day's amount being a half-year's coupon divided by that half-year's days. Its floating rate notes use "a zero-percent minimum daily interest accrual rate".
- _The Factor file's RF counts trading days._ The library: "The Tbill return is the simple daily rate that, over the number of trading days compounds to 1-month TBill rate." So RF has no weekend bump (computed here: RF on a Monday after a weekend averages 0.01157% against 0.01142% on other days).

**What the recipe earns, against real funds (computed here).**

Recipe A, the one to test: each Business day, Return = −Duration × (today's yield − yesterday's yield) + yesterday's yield × (calendar days since the previous Business day) / 365, with the yield of the tenor the fund follows (the catalog's: 2, 5, 7 and 20 years) and the catalog's published duration (1.85, 4.83, 5.77, 13.57). It is compounded over the same window as State Street's published NAV total returns (as of 2026-09-30, "assume the reinvestment of dividends and capital gains", net of fees). The Treasury's yields for 2016 to 2026 come from its yearly CSVs; no split days (a one-day NAV jump above 25%) are in a window except where dropped.

The recipe is within one point a year of the published return in 13 of 16 cells; in the 12 cells of the three Treasury funds it is within 0.65 except the long fund's 5-year cell (+1.03). The three cells beyond a point are the Aggregate fund's 1-year and 3-year and that one. That long-fund cell, where the recipe fell less than the real fund, fits the gap the #46 comment records: the real fund moved about 15.99× the 20-year yield's change, more than the published duration of 13.57 that the recipe keeps. The Aggregate fund runs 0.2 to 1.3 points below in every window, as it should: it holds mortgage and corporate bonds that pay more than Treasuries. A day count of 252 Business days a year instead of calendar days / 365 changes each cell by 0.05 or less, and averaging yesterday's and today's yield changes it by at most 0.01 (computed here); both reach the same total because a year has about 252 Business days and 365 calendar days.

Over the committed-yield window (2019-12-31 to 2026-08-31), the carry of recipe A is 2.87, 2.86, 2.97 and 3.35 %/y for the four funds, against an average tenor yield of 2.85, 2.88, 3.00 and 3.52. The fund's own NAV price return (what is left after the fund pays its dividends out) differs from the recipe's carry-free price return by about 0.26, 0.64, 0.22 and 0.27 points a year: the real NAV fell a little less than −Duration × the yield change. The catalog's intercepts say the same: 0.012, 0.064, 0.057 and 0.024 % a month, or 0.14 to 0.77 % a year. **So a price that follows −Duration × the yield change alone, with the carry paid out as dividends, tracks real fund NAVs to within about 0.2 to 0.65 points a year.**

On 2026-09-30 the funds' 30-day SEC yields (a standard figure for a fund's recent income, as a yearly percentage: 4.57, 4.92, 5.08 and 5.60) were below the tenor's par yield (4.88, 5.09, 5.19, 5.68) by 0.08 to 0.31 points: using the par yield as the fund's yield overstates its income slightly, and ignores the fund's 0.03% fee.

**How it pairs with #47's dividends.** The price rule is previous × (1 + Return) − dividend, and the Return here already counts carry. So price + dividends grows at exactly the Return (the primer's Part 6 argument). Two consequences:

- A bond fund's dividend must be about the carry it has accrued since its last Ex-dividend date: the sum, over those Business days, of each day's previous price × its carry rate. Then the price shows only −Duration × the yield change and own noise, as the real NAVs do. A fixed dividend breaks this: it would drain the price when yields were lower than it assumes and inflate it when higher. Computed here, arithmetic: a 5-year-yield fund that paid out the 4.49% of 31 August 2026 all through 2020 to 2026, when the yield averaged 2.88%, would shed (4.49 − 2.88) × 6.7 ≈ 11 points of price.
- Interest and dividends can't be counted twice, because the dividend only moves value from price to cash.
- A balanced fund is both. Its Return holds its Treasury weight × the bond carry, so its dividend must pay that part out too: its Treasury weight × the carry accrued since its last Ex-dividend date, on top of whatever its stock part pays. If it paid only a stock-style schedule, that carry would stay in the price, about 1.8% a year of upward drift at the 40% weight and 2.8% at the 60% weight, with the 7-year yield at 4.62% (computed here: weight × yield). A real balanced fund pays that income out.

**Does RF play the same role for a stock fund?** Yes for the cash part, no for dividends. A Factor Security's Return is RF plus its exposures times the Factor returns, so RF is the return on cash that every Security starts from, as carry is for a bond fund. The stock fund's dividends are inside Mkt-RF, so there is no second income term, and the stock fund's dividends (#47) are only taken off the price. (The library says "monthly returns are compounded daily returns with dividends reinvested on their ex-dates". That sentence is about monthly returns; that the daily ones count dividends follows from it, but it doesn't say so itself.) **Recommendation for #46.**

- A Treasury-driven Security's Return each Business day is **−Duration × (today's yield − the previous Business day's yield) + the previous Business day's yield × calendar days since the previous Business day ÷ 365** (366 in a leap year, as #47's sweep does), using the yield of the tenor it follows. A blend fund does the same on its Treasury part (tenor 7, 5.77) and weights it by 0.40 or 0.60.
- Use the previous day's yield (known at the previous Close), so no day's carry looks ahead.
- The bond fund's dividend (#47) is the carry accrued since its last Ex-dividend date, computed in the same loop, not a stored amount per share. A balanced fund's includes its Treasury weight × that carry.
- Evidence against: the recipe misses a credit spread (the Aggregate fund), prices every fund off one yield, and uses the par yield, not the fund's own. Each is within the 0.1 to 1.3 point band above and is labelled a simplification.
- For a stock fund the RF term after the file ends is the 1-month yield's carry. Before it ends it is the file's RF.

## Question 3: How is a Security's own surprise drawn?

**What the evidence says about daily residuals.**

- _Fat tails exist in daily Returns, and come mostly from changing volatility._ ABDE2001's introduction: "although the unconditional daily return distributions are leptokurtic, the daily returns normalized by the realized standard deviations are also close to normal", for "the vast majority of the 30 volatilities and 435 covariances/correlations" of the 30 Dow Jones stocks. Fat tails largely disappear once you divide each day by that same day's measured volatility, which can't be known in advance.
- _For single stocks the tails are power-law._ Plerou et al. 1999: for "time scales from 5 min up to approximately 16 days, we find that the tails of the distributions can be well described by a power-law decay, characterized by an exponent α ≈ 3 — well outside the stable Lévy regime 0 < α < 2". An exponent of 3 means a variance but no kurtosis: a Student-t with 3 degrees of freedom would do. It measures whole Returns of 16,000 US companies (1962 to 1996), not residuals after the Factors.
- No study of a Factor-model residual's t degrees of freedom was found that could be read. Fama (1965), Mandelbrot (1963), Blattberg and Gonedes (1974), Bollerslev (1987) and Cont (2001) were not opened (paywalled or blocked; see "Still open").

**Recommendation for #46.**

- Draw each Security's own surprise **once per Business day, from one Student-t distribution with 5 degrees of freedom, scaled to unit variance, then multiplied by own_volatility ÷ √252** (the annual figure divided by the square root of 252). Cap the draw at ±8 daily standard deviations (which lowers its variance from 1 to 0.9935, harmlessly). A balanced fund draws **once**, from its one own volatility, as the catalog says.
- This is a **labelled simplification between three pieces of evidence**: diversified fund residuals measured here are near normal (excess kurtosis 0.7 to 1.6 for the six broad stock funds, which equals ν of 8 to 12), diversified industry portfolios between 4.1 and 8.2 (ν of about 4.7 to 5.5), and single stocks have power-law tails near ν of 3 (not measured here as residuals). ν = 5 has finite kurtosis, a day beyond 5 daily standard deviations about once in 750 days per Security (computed here, exact t integral: 0.0013), and for a fund whose own volatility is 0.01 a 5-standard-deviation day is only 0.3%. A normal would never produce one.
- Evidence against: the six broad stock funds' kurtosis says a normal is nearer for funds. And own noise drawn this way has no volatility clustering, which is where real fat tails come from (ABDE2001); a per-block volatility multiplier would add it, at a cost nothing here asks for.
- Stay with √252 for stocks. For Treasury funds the daily draw at 0.025 ÷ √252 gives the right spread over a month but under-states the real day-to-day wobble by about half; label that.
- **Must a draw be bounded?** Yes, and the bound is enough. The cap of ±8 daily standard deviations is 14% for the most volatile company (CPLN: 0.281 ÷ √252 = 1.77% a day). The lowest day Return a factor-built Security gets from the 2020 to 2026 pool is −13.6% (TMRE, 2020-03-09); with a −8σ own draw it is −26.8% for the worst case (QLNC). The price can't reach zero: `1 + Return` is at least 0.73. The code should still check that every price is above zero and fail loudly, instead of clipping it. The dividend term can't change that while dividends stay under 25% of the price (#47's scope).
- **Can the draw be made in `Decimal`?** Yes, with only arithmetic, `Decimal.sqrt` and `Decimal.ln`, no sine, cosine or exponential. Tested here: `stream("<seed>:own:<date>:<identifier>")` gives the uniform numbers, each `random()` turned into a `Decimal` exactly (a float is an exact binary fraction, so no float arithmetic happens); Marsaglia's polar method turns pairs of them into normals (v1 = 2u1−1 and v2 = 2u2−1; if 0 < v1² + v2² < 1 the normal is v1 × √(−2 ln s ÷ s) with s = v1² + v2², else draw the next pair from the same stream, which keeps the rejection loop deterministic); a Student-t with 5 degrees of freedom is one normal ÷ √(a sum of five squared normals ÷ 5), times √(3/5) for unit variance. Six normals a draw, about 210 microseconds in `Decimal` at a precision of 40 digits, or about 6 seconds for 18 Securities over all 1,700 days since 2020 (40,000 draws; sample variance 1.02; share of |draw| > 5: 0.0015; same inputs gave the same output). Box-Muller needs sine and cosine, which `Decimal` doesn't have.
- **The `Decimal` precision has to be pinned.** A `Decimal` result depends on the context it's calculated in: one draw is −0.2570374417705208740143618975919447216435 at a precision of 40 digits and −0.25703744177052087401436189759194472164384375432688 at 50 (computed here). So every draw and price runs under one context, set in one place, with a fixed precision (40 digits worked here) and half-even rounding, and a test holds a draw's exact value. Changing that precision is a rebuild of the world.

## Question 4: How long should the blocks be?

**Literature.** PW2004 reviews the moving-blocks bootstrap of Künsch (1989) and Liu and Singh (1992) and two cases of a block-length distribution: "(A) The distribution Fb is a unit mass on the positive integer b; this is the circular bootstrap (CB) of Politis and Romano (1992)" (a fixed length) and "(B) The distribution Fb is a Geometric distribution with mean equal to the real number b; this is the stationary bootstrap (SB) of Politis and Romano (1994)". It compares them, with Lahiri (1999), and gives an automatic length choice, which Patton, Politis and White (2009) corrected. Only PW2004 and PPW2009 were read; Künsch, Lahiri and Politis-Romano could not be opened (see "Still open").

What PPW2009 states (read): the stationary bootstrap's variance constant is "D_SB = 2g²(0)"; the ratio of the circular bootstrap's best mean squared error to the stationary one's is printed as 0.7631428 (in the extracted text its formula reads "(2/3)(2/3)", the exponent lost), so at its best length the stationary bootstrap has about 1.3 times the mean squared error of the fixed-length one; "the Politis/White block selection algorithms work exceptionally well": estimated lengths are "between 90% and 110% of the respective true optimal block sizes" on an AR(1) series (a series where each value depends only on the one before; its own Tables 2 and 3 list averages of 0.86 to 1.14). PW2004's algorithm (Section 4), as I coded it: find m, the lag after which the autocorrelations are negligible (below 2√(log₁₀ N / N) for the next K_N = max(5, √log₁₀ N) lags); set M = 2m; estimate G = Σ λ(k/M)|k|R(k) and g(0) = Σ λ(k/M)R(k) over |k| ≤ M with a trapezoid window λ; then b_SB = (2G²/D_SB)^(1/3) N^(1/3) and b_CB = (2G²/D_CB)^(1/3) N^(1/3), with D_SB = 2g²(0) and D_CB = (4/3)g²(0). It is aimed at the variance of a sample mean, not at keeping volatility clustering.

**Check of my implementation (computed here).** On simulated AR(1) series (300 each), the mean of the estimated length over the true optimum was 0.89 (ρ = 0.7, N = 800), 0.80 (ρ = 0.7, N = 200) and 0.85 (ρ = 0.1, N = 800), against 0.93, 0.86 and 0.88 in PPW2009's Table 2: close, a little low. The `arch` package's `optimal_block_length` gives the same numbers for squares and absolute values within 3 and the same size for raw values, except the raw Mkt-RF (mine 2.3, `arch` 6.8), where the sum G is near zero and a one-lag difference in m moves the answer.

## Question 5: Correcting the pool's skew

**The brief's numbers, rerun (computed here).** The pool's Factor means, annualised (arithmetic daily mean × 252) over the 1,674 file days of 2020-01-01 to 2026-08-31: Mkt-RF 13.70%, SMB −1.55%, HML 1.85%, RF 2.89%; over the whole pinned file: 7.84%, 0.97%, 3.79%, 3.01%; since July 1963: 7.33%, 1.11%, 3.71%, 4.32%. All reproduce. Yields rose 0.35 (1-month), 0.43 (5-year, 7-year) and 0.46 (20-year) points a year. A resampling simulation (L = 63, 4,000 paths, floats; my draws differ from the brief's): undemeaned, the 20-year yield's median 10 years out is 9.9% (the brief had 10.11%); demeaned and floored, 5.26% (the brief had 5.50%; the floor isn't the cause, as the 20-year yield almost never reaches it); the 1-month yield goes below zero at some point in 31% of 10-year demeaned paths (25% with L = 21; the brief had 27%). Small differences, same conclusions.

**Should yield changes be demeaned?** Yes, as a choice: the world then expects no drift in yields, which no source requires, but a world with the pool's drift is plainly wrong, as below.

- With the pool's drift kept, the 20-year yield's p5 to p95 at 10 years is 5.4% to 14.2% (median 9.9%, from 5.24% now) and the 1-month yield's median is 7.2% (from 3.85%): rates nearly double in 10 years, and a long fund's median 10-year total return is 0.5% a year.
- The drift isn't statistically visible in 6.6 years: the 2020 to 2026 Mkt-RF mean has a standard error of 8.1 points (20.8% ÷ √6.6), and for yields the data look like a random walk: the variance of the 5-year yield's changes summed over 1, 5, 21, 63, 126 and 250 days, divided by the sum of the daily variances, is 1.0, 0.89, 0.94, 1.02, 0.89 and 1.01 in the pool and 1.0, 0.98, 0.99, 1.11, 1.12 and 1.06 over 1990 to 2019 (computed here), so there is no sign of mean reversion within a year in either, and a pool of six years can't show one at five years.
- Demeaning subtracts one constant per tenor from each resampled change, so the blocks keep their shape. Demeaned yields keep the 2020 to 2026 volatility and the pool's cross-tenor co-movement.
- **Evidence against / better methods.** A random walk spreads further than history: the standard deviation of the 10-year yield's 10-year change is 2.9 points in the demeaned simulation against 1.7 in 1990 to 2026 (the 5-year change: 2.1 against 1.4), and the demeaned 20-year minus 1-month spread (1.4 points now) has a 10-year p5 of −5.0 points, against a 1990 to 2026 minimum of −1.9 for the 10-year minus 3-month. Mean reversion of the level is the textbook fix (Vasicek 1977, which was not opened beyond its record); computed here with a pull κ (per year) toward each tenor's 1990 to 2026 average at L = 21 and the other adjustments: κ = 0.1 narrows the 20-year yield's 10-year p5 to p95 from 1.1 to 9.7 to 1.9 to 7.6 and the spread's p5 to −2.9; κ = 0.25 to 2.5 to 6.4 and −1.5. The funds' 10-year medians move 0.3 to 0.8 points (the long fund's 4.5%, 4.2% and 3.9%). **No source gives κ or the target, and the pool can't estimate it; I don't recommend it.** Rescaling the changes isn't needed.

**Does a real yield go negative? What is Treasury's floor?** Treasury says it: "At times, financial market conditions, in conjunction with extraordinarily low levels of interest rates, may result in negative yields for some Treasury securities trading in the secondary market." "At such times, Treasury will not restrict the use of prices that correspond to negative yields as inputs to the monotone convex spline method. However, the derived par yield curve from these input prices for the Treasury nominal Constant Maturity Treasury series (CMTs) will be floored at zero. This decision is consistent with Treasury not accepting negative yields in Treasury nominal security auctions." So the published par curve the world copies has a floor of zero, and the committed file's lowest values are 0.00 (computed here: 1-month and 3-month). A near-zero yield is real (the 1-month yield was 0.00 to 0.14 in 2021).

**Should factor returns have their means shifted, and to what?**

- Yes, for the same reason: the pool's Mkt-RF mean (13.58% over its 1,673 days) isn't an expectation, and after 10 years it makes an equity fund's median annual return 18.5% against history's 10.7% (computed below). Shift each of Mkt-RF, SMB and HML by a **constant per day**: (the pinned file's 1926 to 2026 daily mean) − (the pool's daily mean). For Mkt-RF that is −0.0228% a day (about −5.7 points a year); for SMB +2.4 and HML +1.9 points a year (all three on the 1,673-day pool). Block shapes are untouched.
- To what? The long-run source is the Pin itself: the library's own 1926 to 2026 means, 7.84%, 0.97% and 3.79% (computed here). It is a recorded value that moves only when the Pin does, and no second source is needed to rebuild it. Alternatives: since 1963, 7.33%, 1.11%, 3.71%. The sub-periods differ a lot (Mkt-RF: 1926 to 1962 8.5%, 1963 to 1989 4.7%, 1990 to 2019 8.5%, 2020 to 2026 13.7%; standard errors of 2.5 to 3.2 points for the 25 to 40 year ones and 8.1 for 2020 to 2026), so the choice of a long window is a labelled simplification, not a fact; the pool's mean is within 0.71 standard errors of 7.84, so shifting isn't a statistical correction but a choice of what the world should expect.
- Evidence against: a realised average isn't the premium investors expect. Fama and French (2002) argued the realised premium of 1951 to 2000 was above what fundamentals support; I couldn't open it (ResearchGate 403), so it isn't a source here. A lower target would be a judgment call.
- **RF isn't resampled or shifted.** It is the 1-month yield (Question 2); after the file ends the stock fund's RF is the 1-month yield path's carry. The same pool of days then gives RF, the Sweep rate and bond carry one shared level.

**Floors, carry, and the level after the file ends.**

- _Yield level._ Last real yield (31 August 2026, per tenor) plus the cumulative demeaned resampled changes, **with the level floored at zero on every day** (`level = max(0, previous level + change)`). That borrows the Treasury's rule for its published curve; the Treasury's rule doesn't speak to a simulation, so it's a labelled choice. Every use of a yield change then reads the change in the floored level (new level − old level), not the change drawn, so a price never moves for a yield move that didn't happen. A per-tenor floor of zero isn't a distortion of the 20-year yield (it almost never reaches it) and matters for the 1-month, though the world is one path, not an average of them. In the median 10-year path the 1-month yield never gets to 0.5%; in the 90th percentile path it spends 23% of its days there, and in the 99th, 53% (computed here). Pooled across all paths, the share of days is 2.6% at 0.05% or below, 6.0% at 0.5% or below and 9.4% at 1.0% or below.
- _Sweep rate._ The sweep can't pay a negative rate, so whatever #47 subtracts from the 1-month yield needs its own floor at zero. The yield floor is #46's, because bond carry and RF read the same level; the margin below the yield and the sweep's own floor are #47's. The shares above say how often it binds, with a margin of 0.5 or 1.0 point: most worlds never get there, and about one in ten spends a fifth of its days there at a margin of 0.5.
- _Bond income._ A floored yield level makes carry non-negative by itself (carry = previous level × days ÷ 365), so a bond fund's income needs no floor of its own. Bloomberg and Treasury don't state one for fund income; Treasury's own floating rate notes have "a zero-percent minimum daily interest accrual rate", the same idea.

- Demean every tenor's resampled yield change over the pool; floor the level at zero every day; start from the last real yield.

- Say in the docs that the pool's volatility (20.8% for Mkt-RF) is above the long run's (17.1%) and that the 2020 crash is 3% of its days (a labelled simplification; the task is to correct the skew, which this does not touch).

## Question 6: What is a Business day?

**The NYSE's rule, read.** NYSE Rule 7.2 (Holidays), as reproduced in the 2019 rulebook PDF: "The Exchange will not be open for business on New Year's Day, Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents' Day, Good Friday, Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving Day and Christmas Day. When a holiday observed by the Exchange falls on a Saturday, the Exchange will not be open for business on the preceding Friday and when any holiday observed by the Exchange falls on a Sunday, the Exchange will not be open for business on the succeeding Monday, unless unusual business conditions exist, such as the ending of a monthly or yearly accounting period." SR-NYSE-2021-56 (86 FR 55068) adds Juneteenth: "the Exchange will not be open for business on Juneteenth National Independence Day, which falls on June 19 of each year", with the Saturday and Sunday paragraph applying, and says of the exception: "There is an exception to the practice if unusual business conditions exist, such as the ending of a monthly or yearly accounting period."

- The "unless unusual business conditions exist" clause stands after both the Saturday and the Sunday case in the text. In practice only New Year's Day can trigger it, because it is the only holiday on the first of a month: when 1 January is a Saturday the preceding Friday, 31 December, is the last Business day of the year and the Exchange stays open. NYSE's table for 2028 says so: "Because the holiday falls on Saturday, January 1, 2028, no New Year's Day holiday is observed."
- The rule doesn't give the dates of the Monday holidays. They are the federal holidays of 5 U.S.C. § 6103(a): Martin Luther King Jr. Day the third Monday in January, Washington's Birthday (the NYSE's "Presidents' Day") the third Monday in February, Memorial Day the last Monday in May, Labor Day the first Monday in September, Thanksgiving the fourth Thursday in November. This is my reading, confirmed by every date below.
- **Good Friday** is the Friday before Easter. The US Naval Observatory: "Easter falls on the first Sunday following the first ecclesiastical full moon that occurs on or after the day of the vernal equinox", with "The vernal equinox occurs on March 21" and the full moon from the tables of the bull Inter Gravissimas. I computed Easter with the anonymous Gregorian algorithm (Meeus/Jones/Butcher: the algorithm itself wasn't opened, but its output agrees with every Good Friday of 2020 to 2028 below).
- **Juneteenth** has closed the NYSE since 2022 (the rule change was filed on 28 September 2021). The file has a row for Friday 18 June 2021, so 2021 wasn't a holiday; 19 June 2022 fell on a Sunday, and the file has no row for Monday 20 June 2022.
- **One-off closures** come from Rule 7.1(c) and (d): "the CEO of the Exchange or his or her designee may take any of the following actions: … (3) close some or all Exchange facilities", only when "he or she deems such action to be necessary or appropriate for the maintenance of a fair and orderly market, or the protection of investors or otherwise in the public interest, due to extraordinary circumstances such as … (iii) a period of mourning or recognition for a person or event". The closure on 9 January 2025, for the National Day of Mourning for President Carter, is an example: [the research on the Close](the-close.md) reads ICE's release of it. The Factor file has no row for it, and `exchange_calendars` lists it as an ad hoc closure.

**Coded and checked (computed here).** About 80 lines of Python (Easter, the n-th weekday of a month, the Saturday and Sunday shift, the year-end exception, Juneteenth from 2022). Checked against every weekday of 2020-01-01 to 2026-08-31 (1,739 weekdays, 64 predicted closures) and the pinned Factor file's 1,674 dates: the rule predicts the file's days exactly except one, **9 January 2025** (the rule says open; the file has no row), which is the one-off closure above. No other mismatch. It also reproduces NYSE's published tables for 2026, 2027 and 2028 (29 closures, including Friday 3 July 2026, Friday 18 June 2027, Monday 5 July 2027, Friday 24 December 2027 and no 1 January 2028).

**What the bond market does (read).** SIFMA's recommendations: full closes on Columbus Day (Monday 12 October 2026) and Veterans Day (Wednesday 11 November 2026), which the NYSE doesn't observe, and "Early Close (12:00 p.m. Eastern Time): Friday, April 3, 2026" for Good Friday, when the NYSE is shut. That matches the committed yields (computed here): 11 days in the Factor file have no yields (Columbus Day, and Veterans Day when it falls on a weekday: 2020-10-12, 2020-11-11, 2021-10-11, 2021-11-11, 2022-10-10, 2022-11-11, 2023-10-09, 2024-10-14, 2024-11-11, 2025-10-13 and 2025-11-11), and 4 dates have yields but no Factors (2021-04-02, 2023-04-07, 2026-04-03: Good Fridays; 2025-01-09). So the yields' dates are not a Business-day calendar. SIFMA's page for 2021, 2023 and 2025 wasn't read.

**Rule or dependency?** `exchange_calendars` 4.13.2's `XNYS` agrees with the rule on every weekday from 2020 to 2028 except 2025-01-09, which it has as an ad hoc closure (the rule here has no one-offs). Two things against it for this world: by default its calendar ends about a year after today (observed on 2026-10-30: `get_calendar("XNYS")` ended at 2027-10-29 and asking for 2028 failed with `DateOutOfBounds`; an explicit `end` avoids it), so the default range moves with the clock and the world must read no clock; and its ad hoc closures (2018-12-05 and 2025-01-09 in the list read) are data that a release can add to (its changelog wasn't read), so a dependency bump could change a date that already has a stored price. A rule in code, with a golden test, can change only on a commit.

**Recommendation for #46.**

- A **Business day is a date in the pinned Factor file, up to its last date (2026-08-31); after it, a weekday that isn't a closure under Rule 7.2 coded in the custodian** (not a dependency). The proposal to use the file's dates and then the rule stands; the rule is the same one that reproduces the file.
- Test the code against the file (every weekday of 2020 to 2026: only 2025-01-09 may differ, and the file wins) and against NYSE's published 2026 to 2028 list as a golden table. No table of one-off closures is needed: up to 2026-08-31 the file is the authority, and after it no closure can be known in advance.
- Don't use the yields' dates as Business days. Yields are "absent" on Columbus and Veterans Day (change 0, the next day's change spans both) and present on Good Friday (ignore: the next Business day's change spans both).
- One more reason to keep the rule in code: the block index n (Business days after the file's end) is computed from it, so a change of the rule would move every later block.

## What this means for #46 and #47

The behaviour, in order of a Business day:

1. **Calendar.** A date is a Business day if it is in the Factor file (up to 2026-08-31) or, after it, a weekday that Rule 7.2 doesn't close. Business-day index n counts from 0 on the first one after the file's end.
2. **Inputs for a real-history day.** The file's Factor returns, the committed yields. The change in a yield is its value minus the last Business day's; a day with no yield has change 0.
3. **Inputs after the file ends.** One source day per Business day for every input: block k = n // 63, start = a whole number from 0 to 1,672 drawn from `stream("<seed>:block:<k>")`, source day = (start + n mod 63) mod 1,673, in the pool of file days from 2020-01-03. Mkt-RF, SMB, HML and every tenor's yield change come from that same day. Factors are shifted by a constant per day to the file's whole-file means; yield changes have their pool mean removed. Yield level per tenor: max(0, last level + change), starting at the last real yield. The blocks need no state, but the yield level is a running total from the file's end, so it's worked out day by day from there: reproducible, not a formula of the date alone. The same goes for a price (from the previous stored price) and a bond fund's dividend (from the carry since its last Ex-dividend date).
4. **Return per driver.** Factors: RF + exposures × Factors + own surprise (RF from the file, then the 1-month yield's carry). Treasury: −Duration × (new floored level − old floored level) + previous level × days ÷ 365 + own surprise. Blend: weights × (Factors part, Treasury part) + one own surprise. Cash sweep: none, 1.0000 always.
5. **Own surprise.** One draw per Security per Business day: Student-t, 5 degrees of freedom, unit variance, capped at ±8, × own volatility ÷ √252, from `stream("<seed>:own:<date>:<identifier>")`, in `Decimal` with square roots and logarithms only, under one pinned context (precision and half-even rounding).
6. **Price.** previous stored price × (1 + Return) − dividend (on an Ex-dividend date), rounded to 4 places, half to even; checked above zero, never clipped. Prices stay at 4 places for every Security.
7. **Dividends (#47).** A bond fund's dividend is the carry accrued over the days after its last Ex-dividend date, up to and including this one; a balanced fund's includes its Treasury weight × that carry; a stock fund's is a schedule set so that the price drift matches the real fund (not decided here). The Security's Total return is then just the day's Return, as the primer's Part 6 says of a stock fund.
8. **Sweep rate (#47).** From the 1-month yield level (floored at zero by #46); the margin and the sweep's own zero floor are #47's. Most worlds never see a 1-month yield at or below 0.5 point; about one in ten spends a fifth of its days there.
9. **Tests.** The primer's stock table at 4 places and its bond table with carry (recomputed) are the ready-made tests; the calendar is tested against the file and NYSE's 2026 to 2028 table.
