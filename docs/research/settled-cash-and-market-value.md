# How US Custodians Treat Settled Cash, Cancelled Transactions, Missing Prices and Rounded Market Values

> Research notes gathered on 2026-10-24 for issue #84 (Settled cash and Market value) while checking the repo's rules against the real world. They inform the decisions but are not decisions: where this note and an ADR disagree, the ADR wins ([ADR 0013](../adr/0013-money-shares-prices-and-rates-are-separate-types.md), [ADR 0015](../adr/0015-positions-counted-by-trade-date.md)). Every claim below was read in the cited document unless the text says otherwise. Statement samples and agreements are dated in the table where the date is known; several are old or undated.

Terms, once: a **cash account** is a brokerage account where the customer pays for every purchase in full and can't borrow (a **margin account** can). **Regulation T** ("Reg T", 12 CFR part 220) is the Federal Reserve rule on the credit brokers may give customers. A **free credit balance** is cash a broker owes a customer on demand. A **hold period** is the number of days a broker blocks withdrawal of a new deposit while the bank transfer could still bounce. **Freeriding** is buying and selling a security in a cash account without ever paying for the purchase; a **good faith violation** is the milder version, selling a security that was bought with sale proceeds that hadn't settled yet. **Cancel** and **reversal** are FINRA's words for removing a trade from the record: "as a general matter" a cancel on the day it was submitted and a reversal on a later day (some FINRA systems allow a cancel for a few days). **As/of** means a record about an earlier date, written later (the repo calls the day it was written the Posting date). **Unpriced** means the firm has no price for a security on the statement date.

The repo's rule under test: money going out (buy, fee, withdrawal) counts toward Settled cash from its Trade date; money coming in (sale, contribution, dividend) counts only from its Settlement date. A Reversal copies the original row's Trade and Settlement dates with every amount negated, and gets a later Posting date.

## Answer first

**Q1. Settled cash**

1. **The repo's rule is how two large firms define their settled or withdrawable cash; the others differ or are silent.** Fidelity's "Available to withdraw" (the page doesn't say cash or margin account): "Sell orders are reflected in this balance on settlement date and Buy orders are reflected on trade date." Interactive Brokers' "Settled Cash" (a TWS Balances-window figure, not called "withdrawable"): cash recognized at settlement "minus the purchases at time of trade"; its separate "Cash Available for Withdrawal" says that for a cash account part of the balance "represents proceeds from trades that have not yet settled" and is not withdrawable. Vanguard reads differently: its agreement's free credit balance is cash "less what is needed to pay for unsettled purchase transactions", but its help pages say the account is debited for a purchase "on the settlement date", define "Funds available to withdraw" as "the settlement fund balance and settled credits or debits" adjusted for open orders, and warn that withdrawing after a trade but before settlement "can result in having insufficient funds". My reading (inference) is that Vanguard's withdrawable figure does not hold back unsettled buys. Schwab's video transcript (written for margin accounts) says "Settled Funds" is cash from deposits or sales that "have reached the settlement date", and says nothing about purchases. Confidence: **high** for Fidelity and IBKR as definitions, **low-to-medium** that this is the industry norm (two match, Vanguard differs, Schwab is silent on buys).
2. **The trade-date debit for buys is firm policy, not regulation.** Reg T gives a cash-account customer the standard settlement cycle plus two business days to pay (three business days now), and the only sanctions in the text I read are cancel or liquidate, and a 90-day freeze. No regulation I read says withdrawable cash must be net of unsettled buys, or that sale proceeds can't be withdrawn until settlement. The one rule nearby, SEC Rule 15c3-3, defines a free credit balance as what is "subject to immediate cash payment to customers on demand". Confidence: **medium-high**; I did not read FINRA's rules on withdrawals.
3. **No custodian I could open says how a cancelled or reversed row affects withdrawable cash.** What the sources do show: the withdrawable formulas never include an unsettled sale, so a cancelled unsettled sale leaves nothing to take back; FINRA's reporting FAQ treats a cancel as removing the trade from the (public trade) record, not as a new opposite trade; Reg T says a cancellation "other than to correct an error" counts as a sale for the 90-day freeze; and contracts say a returned deposit may be removed or debited and that the firm may correct errors by reversing transactions (Vanguard adds that the customer is "not entitled to retain any benefit resulting from an error"). Read together (an inference), a cancel before settlement leaves withdrawable cash as if the trade never happened, and a reversal after settlement is a debit when the firm acts on it (it can leave the account negative if the money was already withdrawn). Confidence: **medium** for the before-settlement case (an inference from formulas), **medium** for the after-settlement debit (three agreements, Fidelity's, Apex's and Vanguard's, say a returned deposit may be removed or charged; only Vanguard's covers errors; none says the debit is dated the day it posts).
4. **The plain sign rule gets the before-settlement case wrong.** Applied row by row, a Reversal of an unsettled sale (negative, so "out", counts at once) lowers settled cash by the sale amount until the original would have settled. A Reversal of a buy (positive, so "in", waits) leaves settled cash low until settlement. A returned deposit hits the same way. Worked numbers are in Q1 below; they come from applying the repo's rule, not from a source.
5. **Other edges real firms have that the sign rule doesn't:** a deposit is usually credited before it can be withdrawn (hold periods of three business days at IBKR for wire or bank-check deposits, "up to six business days" in Fidelity's Cash Management Account agreement, seven calendar days at Vanguard for bank transfers and checks); trading power and withdrawable cash are different numbers (unsettled sale proceeds can buy but not be withdrawn); open orders reduce cash before any trade (Fidelity, Vanguard); a dividend can be reversed after it was paid (Robinhood says so; DTC's 2012 order limits its settlement-system adjustments to 90 calendar days after the payment date from January 2015, and I did not check later changes). Confidence: **high** that each exists; the dividend time limit is dated 2012.

**Q2. Market value with no price on the day**

6. **A weekend, holiday or stale date uses the last known price.** This is an inference from thin evidence. Fidelity's live-balances help page (not a statement): "if the current market price is unavailable or the market is closed", it uses the most recent closing value. SEC Rule 15g-6 (penny stocks only) puts the statement's information "as of the last trading day of the period", and its value is the highest bid on that day. Rule 22c-1 requires a fund's price only Monday to Friday and not on listed holidays; it doesn't say what a custodian shows on other days. Vanguard's pre-June-2016 statement guide says prices "reflect quotations on the statement date". No source gives a maximum age for a carried-forward price, and none says in terms what a statement dated on a weekend shows. Confidence: **medium** that it's the practice, **low** that any rule requires it for ordinary securities: FINRA Rule 2231 (read in full) has no valuation requirement for them.
7. **No price at all: show no value, leave it out of the total, say so.** Four firms agree, in sources from 2005 to 2025 (Schwab's is a hypothetical 2005 sample, Fidelity's a 2015 sample, Vanguard's a guide for statements before June 2016, Fidelity's FAQ is from 2025, UBS's page is undated). Vanguard marks it "—" and it is "omitted from the total". Fidelity shows "Unavailable" or "---" and the total "Excludes unpriced securities". Schwab's sample statement has an "Unpriced Securities" section with "N/A" and a "Total Account Value (excl. Unpriced Securities)". UBS (online display): "Your account value excludes unpriced securities". Nobody shows $0. SEC Rule 15g-6 points the same way for penny stocks only: a statement that there is "no estimated market value". Confidence: **medium-high** (consistent, but most of the samples are old).
8. **A date before the first price** is not covered by any source I read; the closest case is "no price", which is rule 7. Confidence: **low** (inference).

**Q3. Rounding market value on a statement**

9. **Every statement I read shows each position's value to the cent, and none states how the total relates to the lines.** Vanguard: balances "displayed in short form using only two decimal places". Fidelity's 2015 sample statement shows cents per line, and its fine print says "The Total Market Value has been calculated out to 9 decimal places; however, the individual unit price is displayed in 5 decimal places." That shows higher precision underneath, but the fine print doesn't say whether "Total Market Value" means a position's value (quantity times price) or the account total, and it doesn't say whether the printed total is the sum of the printed lines. Confidence: **high** for cents per line, **low** for line-sum versus unrounded total.
10. **No rule or standard sets it.** FINRA Rule 2231, Supplementary Material .08(b), asks only that a summary statement's aggregate be "recognizable as having been arithmetically derived from the separately stated totals or their components" (and only where a member and another firm jointly provide statements with a summary of assets held in different accounts). Fidelity admits that "due to rounding, percentages may not add to 100%", but I found no such line for dollar totals. The only formal add-then-round rule I found is the IRS's for tax forms (include cents when adding, "only round off the total"), which is not a statement rule. Confidence: **medium** that nothing sets it (I could not search every disclosure).

### What the sources show

**(a) Is the repo's rule how custodians define it?** For withdrawable cash, yes, closely. Fidelity and IBKR state it in the repo's terms (sales at settlement, buys at trade). Vanguard is less clear: the agreement's free credit balance reserves "what is needed to pay for unsettled purchase transactions", but the help pages debit the account for a purchase on the settlement date and define "Funds available to withdraw" as the settlement fund balance and settled credits or debits, adjusted for open orders; they also warn that withdrawing before a purchase settles "can result in having insufficient funds". Schwab's transcript describes settled funds as deposits and sales that have reached settlement, and doesn't mention purchases. A naming trap: Fidelity's "Settled cash" is another number (what you can trade without a good faith violation, and it adds proceeds that settle today), and Fidelity's "Available to withdraw" is the match for the repo's Settled cash. IBKR's "Settled Cash" matches by name.

**Regulation versus policy.** Regulation: the settlement cycle (Rule 15c6-1, one business day), the cash-account payment period of three business days (Reg T), cancel or liquidate when payment is late, and the 90-day freeze after selling something that wasn't paid for. Firm policy: the withdrawal rule itself, hold periods, the warnings-then-restriction ladder for good faith violations (described on Schwab's and Vanguard's pages, not in the Reg T text I read), and counting a buy against cash on trade date. The trade-date count is stricter than Reg T needs, because Reg T would allow paying on the third business day.

**(b) Cancels and reversals.** Three kinds of evidence, none of them a statement that a custodian restates withdrawable cash a particular way:

- **The formulas.** A sale is not in "Available to withdraw" or "Settled Cash" until its settlement date. A trade that is cancelled before that date never reaches the settlement date, so there is nothing to take back. Likewise a buy is reserved from the trade date; a cancelled buy is no longer a buy. This is an inference from Fidelity's and IBKR's wording, not a quote.
- **What cancel means.** FINRA's reporting FAQ treats a cancel as taking the trade off the record, and a later cancel as a reversal reported as/of. It is about public trade reports, not customer cash.
- **After the money has moved.** A returned ACH deposit is removed or charged (Fidelity, Apex, Vanguard), even if the customer already traded on it (Schwab's example, DriveWealth's checklist). A wrong dividend is reversed and the customer's account is adjusted (DTC filing, Robinhood). Nothing says the reversal reaches back and changes balances on earlier days; the contracts describe a debit when the firm finds out. Fidelity's late-dividend note shows the other half: a late record keeps the original date and shows up on a later statement.

**(c) Other edges.** Deposits: credited, but not withdrawable for 3 business days (IBKR, wire or bank check), up to 6 (Fidelity's Cash Management Account agreement), 7 calendar days (Vanguard, bank transfer or check); a bounce can come after the hold starts, so the return can arrive before the money ever became withdrawable (my inference; Schwab's example has the return a day after the trade). Trading versus withdrawing: unsettled sale proceeds can fund a purchase, so a sign rule applied naively makes Settled cash negative (see below); the sources don't say whether firms floor "available to withdraw" at zero. Open orders: Fidelity's "Available to trade" falls when a buy order is placed, before any trade (the repo has no orders). Margin accounts let customers use unsettled funds (Schwab), so these rules are for cash accounts; Fidelity's "Available to withdraw" and IBKR's "Settled Cash" definitions don't say which account type they describe. Failed trades: a late sale delays the cash (the repo primer already says this). Money market and sweep: Fidelity counts the core fund in "Available to withdraw".

### Worked numbers: the plain sign rule against "as if it never happened"

Rows are from Dana's week where they exist ([primer](../learn/01-ledger-positions-and-dates.md)); the deposit row is hypothetical. These are my arithmetic on the repo's rule, not source facts. "Plain" means each row counted by its own sign; "never happened" means the original and its Reversal cancel on every date. The Reversal copies the original's Trade and Settlement dates and is posted on the day shown.

| Case                                                                                       | Reversal posted | End of day | Plain sign rule | As if never happened |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------- | ---------- | --------------- | -------------------- |
| Sale +1,520.00, Trade Wed 4 Mar, Settles Thu 5 Mar; Reversal −1,520.00                     | Wed 4 Mar       | Wed 4 Mar  | −1,520.00       | 0.00                 |
|                                                                                            |                 | Thu 5 Mar  | 0.00            | 0.00                 |
| Buy −6,010.00, Trade Mon 2 Mar, Settles Tue 3 Mar; Reversal +6,010.00                      | Mon 2 Mar       | Mon 2 Mar  | −6,010.00       | 0.00                 |
|                                                                                            |                 | Tue 3 Mar  | 0.00            | 0.00                 |
| Deposit +10,000.00, Trade Mon, Settles Fri after a hold; ACH returned, Reversal −10,000.00 | Wed             | Wed, Thu   | −10,000.00      | 0.00                 |
|                                                                                            |                 | Fri        | 0.00            | 0.00                 |

The "plain" column is the change in Settled cash from the pair alone. In each case the pair is a dip that lasts from the Reversal's Posting date to the original's Settlement date, and then disappears. Once both dates have passed the two readings agree, so the question only bites for a Reversal posted before the original settles, which is the usual case for a trade bust, a same-week correction and an ACH return.

After the original has settled, both readings agree: the Reversal takes the amount back on the day it posts. If the customer had already withdrawn it, Settled cash ends below where it started and can go negative (Apex: "may charge my account"; Vanguard: "may debit Your Account").

### What this means for #84

A faithful Settled cash is the Fidelity/IBKR shape: out counts from Trade date, in counts from Settlement date. The sources don't fix how a Reversal enters it, so what follows is inference. A faithful simulator would give a Reversal posted before its original settles **no net effect on withdrawable cash at any date** (the pair cancels on every date), and would give a Reversal posted after settlement the effect of a plain debit or credit from its Posting date. Confidence: **medium** (formulas and contract language agree; no firm states it). Applying the sign rule row by row breaks the first half in the three cases above, so that is a choice between pairing a Reversal with the row it names, or taking the direction (in or out) from the original row. That choice is engineering, not domain: the sources cannot make it. Two product choices the sources leave open: whether a deposit's Settlement date means "booked" or "withdrawable after the hold" (real firms have both, with different dates), and whether Settled cash is floored at zero.

### What the sources show

**Non-trading days.** No source describes a weekend in the simple terms of the repo's question; what follows is my reading. Fidelity's live-balances page values a closed market at the last close (it is not about statements). SEC Rule 15g-6 ties a penny-stock statement to "the last trading day of the period", so a period ending on a Sunday would be valued on the Friday's quotation, but the rule doesn't say that in terms. Rule 22c-1 requires a fund NAV only Monday to Friday (with exceptions), so on a Saturday a fund's last NAV is likely the only one, though the rule doesn't forbid a weekend NAV and I did not check whether any fund strikes one. FINRA Rule 2231 (read in full) and the GIPS Q&As I opened say nothing on the point.

**No price at all.** The pattern is the same at Vanguard, Fidelity and Schwab: the line gets a marker (not a number), and the total is stated to leave the line out; UBS's page states the exclusion only. Two of them (Schwab, Fidelity's sample) also footnote the total. Nobody values an unpriced security at $0, and Fidelity says outright that a missing price means the value "cannot be calculated". Money market and sweep lines print at $1.00 in the Schwab and Fidelity samples.

**Stale and too-early prices.** No source gives a limit on how old a carried-forward price may be. UBS says only that "Certain security prices are not updated daily". Nothing I read speaks to a date before a security's first price.

**A side finding for ADR 0015.** Vanguard's statement guide values positions on "a trade-date basis at the close of the statement period", the same basis the repo chose for Positions.

### What this means for #84

A faithful Market value on a date with no price of its own would carry the **last known price on or before that date**: Friday's close on a Saturday, and the last close after the final price in the data. Confidence: **medium**, an inference (Fidelity's live-balances page, Vanguard's old "quotations on the statement date", Rule 15g-6's last-trading-day date; none says what a statement dated on a weekend shows). With **no price on or before the date** (a date before the first price, or a security that never had one), a faithful simulator would show **no value rather than $0** and leave that Position out of the total, with the total marked as excluding it. Confidence: **medium-high** for the no-price case (four firms, but the Schwab, Fidelity and Vanguard sources are old samples or guides), **low** for treating "before the first price" as that case, since no source covers it. Left open (product choices, not domain facts): how stale a carried-forward price may become before the value should be treated as unavailable (no source has a limit), and how the total should be labelled so it can't be mistaken for complete.

### What the sources show

**Lines.** Statements print each position's value to the cent (Vanguard says so in words; Fidelity's sample and Robinhood's addendum show it). Fidelity's fine print says a "Total Market Value" is carried to 9 decimal places, and its sample's lines agree with quantity times price rounded at the cent.

**Totals.** No firm states whether the printed total is the sum of the printed lines or the rounded sum of unrounded values. Fidelity's "calculated out to 9 decimal places" sentence is about a "Total Market Value" that the page does not define. If it means the account total, that is some evidence that totals are built from unrounded values; if it means a position's value, it says nothing about totals. Either way it does not speak to the printed lines and total together. In the Fidelity sample every section total happens to equal the sum of its printed lines, but its quantities and prices leave almost no sub-cent remainders, so it can't tell the two methods apart (I checked the mutual fund total and the stock totals): its one line with a sub-cent remainder, 11,361.52032, can't change the cents of a sum. Schwab's sample foots but isn't real arithmetic. Fidelity warns that percentages may not add to 100% because of rounding, and I found no similar warning for dollars.

**Rules.** No SEC or FINRA rule sets the convention. FINRA's summary-statement text (Rule 2231, Supplementary Material .08(b)) is the nearest, and it asks that an aggregate be traceable to the separate totals or their components, which is a footing expectation across accounts, not within one list of holdings. The IRS rule is a tax-form rule and rounds half up ("50 to 99 cents" go up), which differs from half-to-even.

### How this squares with ADR 0013

ADR 0013's rule is "round once, when it becomes recorded Money", half-to-even for aggregates. Rounding each position's Market value to the cent when it is shown fits what statements print on every line. Computing a total from full-precision values and rounding it once is consistent with Fidelity's disclosure (if "Total Market Value" there means the account total, which is unclear) and fits the IRS add-then-round-the-total rule. The two together mean a printed total can differ by a cent or two from the sum of printed lines, and the sources show that happening is tolerated for percentages but give no example for dollars. Half-to-even versus half-up: no statement source states a tie-breaking rule, and the one formal rule I found (IRS) rounds half up, so the sources neither support nor contradict half-to-even here.

### What this means for #84

A faithful statement shows each Position's Market value rounded to the cent and a total that is **either** the sum of those lines **or** the rounded exact sum; the sources do not choose between them. Confidence: **high** for cents per line, **low** (no source) for which total. Whether a report should foot to the cent against its own lines is a product choice: footing makes the page easy to check by hand, while round-once keeps the total exact (ADR 0013) and can differ from the lines' sum by up to a cent per line. The primer's Part 5 example (14,084.65) can't show the difference, since its prices give whole cents. The tie-breaking mode (half-even or half-up) is also open: no custodian source speaks to it, and ADR 0013 already decides it for the repo.
