# How US Brokers and Funds Turn Dollars into Recorded Fractional Shares

> Research notes gathered on 2026-10-24 while choosing the rounding rule for recorded Shares in the simulated Custodian. They inform the decisions but are not decisions: where this note and an ADR disagree, the ADR wins ([ADR 0013](../adr/0013-money-shares-prices-and-rates-are-separate-types.md)). Every claim below was read in the cited document unless the text says otherwise.

Terms, once: a **Custodian** is the firm that holds the assets and keeps the official record of who owns what (the book of record). A **DRIP** (dividend reinvestment plan) turns a cash dividend into more shares of the same security. A **transfer agent** keeps a fund's or company's list of shareholders and their share counts. **NAV** (net asset value) is a fund's price per share, computed once a day. **Truncation** means dropping the digits past the last one you keep (0.4449818 becomes 0.444981); **rounding to nearest** looks at the next digit (it becomes 0.444982).

## Answer first

1. **Direction: round down (truncate).** Every firm that says which way it goes says down: Fidelity, Schwab, Pershing (which clears for other brokers), and FINRA's own reporting rule. None states round-to-nearest for a dollar purchase. Vanguard and Robinhood say "rounded" without a direction, so I can't tell which they mean. Confidence: **medium-high** for brokerage dollar orders, **medium** for DRIPs specifically, because only Pershing explicitly applies its round-down convention to DRIPs.
2. **Decimals: no single standard.** Three (Fidelity, Vanguard DRIP, Apex DRIP, ICI's mutual fund norm), four (Schwab, Pershing), five (TD, Canadian), six (Robinhood, many company DRIPs, FINRA's trade tape). Six is the most common ceiling for newer systems and the only one set by a regulator-side rule, and that rule is for reporting trades, not for recording holdings.
3. **Cash side: the customer is left with the residue, and shares x price is what the trade costs.** Schwab says truncation leaves a buy "$99.98 instead of $100". The leftover stays as cash. No source says the firm keeps it. No source says the cash debit is forced to equal the original dollar amount.
4. **No rule sets this.** SEC Rule 22c-1 fixes the price a fund must use (NAV) and says nothing about decimals or rounding. I found no SEC rule, DTCC document or FINRA notice that says how many places a holding must have or which way to round it. The Fund/SERV record layouts that would show the share precision are behind a DTCC login.

## What the sources show

**Direction.** The explicit statements all point down. ICI, the one source on mutual fund transfer agents, says the choice "is often based on a system's core design", so funds are mixed. It warns that a displayed 1.003 could stand for anything from 1.00250 to 1.00399, so parties using different methods cannot reconcile.

**Decimals.** Funds sit at three. Brokers sit at three to six. Company DRIP plans state their own number and rarely say which way the last digit goes.

**Cash.** Where a source describes it, the trade costs shares x execution price and the small remainder stays in the customer's cash. Robinhood trades as principal and rounds money to the cent, a different model from an agency broker. Nobody documents round-to-nearest, so I found nothing on who keeps its residue.

**Regulation.** FINRA's rule is the nearest thing to a standard: trade reports carry up to six decimals, truncated, from 2026-02-23. Its notice also says NSCC (the clearing house) "does not currently support the clearing of fractional share trades", so each broker settles fractions inside its own books and sets its own convention.

## What this means for ADR 0013

**The evidence supports 0.444981 (truncate), not 0.444982.** 37.50 / 84.2731 = 0.44498185..., and every source that names a direction names down.

| Rule             | Shares   | Shares x price | Against the $37.50 |
| ---------------- | -------- | -------------- | ------------------ |
| Truncate         | 0.444981 | $37.4999283    | $0.0000717 under   |
| Round to nearest | 0.444982 | $37.5000126    | $0.0000126 over    |

Why truncate, in plain terms:

- **A book of record must not invent value.** Round-to-nearest sometimes records shares worth more than the cash that bought them (here, by about a hundredth of a cent). Truncation can only ever leave the customer slightly under-invested, never over. Schwab, Pershing and Fidelity chose the same direction.
- **Six places matches FINRA's reporting field.** A Custodian that records six places and truncates can hand its numbers to a trade report with no further conversion. The report truncates at six too.

Two decisions this leaves open, which are the owner's:

1. **Cash debit.** The sources support "cash out = shares x price" with the leftover staying in cash. Here the unrounded cost is $37.4999283, which becomes $37.50 at the cent, so debiting $37.50 and debiting shares x price give the same recorded Money. That holds whenever the price is under about $5,000, because one unit in the sixth place is worth less than half a cent below that price. This is my inference from the arithmetic, not something a firm states. Above that price, shares x price and the dollar amount can differ by a cent, and a rule is needed.
2. **Where the residue goes.** With truncation the sub-cent remainder is simply not invested. No source describes carrying it forward to the next dividend (a search summary mentioned one Australian Computershare plan that does; I did not open it).

## What I could not verify

- **Fund/SERV (DTCC's mutual fund order network) share precision.** The record layouts are login-only on dtcclearning.com. No DTCC, NSCC or SEC document I could open states it.
- **Mutual fund prospectuses or SAIs** stating share precision or direction. I found only the ICI note, a 2005 supplement, and closed-end plans.
- **Fidelity's and Schwab's actual DRIP direction.** Fidelity's page covers orders, and Schwab's terms cover Stock Slices purchases. Neither says reinvestment follows the same rule. Pershing is the only one that links the two.
- **Interactive Brokers and current Robinhood terms.** The IBKR pages and the current Robinhood agreement did not load.
- **Document dates.** The Schwab terms were read from an archived copy because the live URL blocks scripts; the date is unknown. The ICI note is dated 6/30/15 and is written for money market funds; Fidelity's page is educational, not a contract.
